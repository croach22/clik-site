# Why Your Podcast Clips Look Generic (It's Not the Captions)

Here's a loop most teams have run at least once.

The clips aren't performing. So you change the caption font. Then the caption animation: word-by-word pop instead of the karaoke fill. Then you tighten the crop, add a punch-in every four seconds, try the outline everyone's using this month. You ship the next batch. The numbers look the same.

The conclusion people draw is that the styling still isn't right. It usually is. The problem is that styling was never the variable.

## What actually separates a clip that works

Watch clips from a show that consistently performs and time how long you're looking at the person talking.

It's less than you'd guess. The clip opens on the speaker, holds for a few seconds while the hook lands, and then it **cuts away**. To the product they just named. To footage of the event they're describing. To the screen they're referencing. The dashboard, the tweet, the chart. To a brand graphic that states the number out loud. To a moment from a past episode when they said the opposite thing.

Then it comes back to the face. Then it cuts away again.

That's the whole difference. A clip that cuts away is a piece of content. A clip that doesn't is a recording of someone talking, cropped to vertical, with nice text on it.

This isn't a point about attention spans or about dialogue being boring. Dialogue is the entire substance of the format. The words are why anyone is there. Cutting away is what makes the words land. When someone says "we grew from 400 customers to 12,000 in a year," showing the line going up doesn't distract from the sentence. It makes the sentence hit twice.

## The structural problem

Now look at where all of that material lives.

The product shot is in a folder from a photoshoot last spring. The event footage is on a card from a conference in March. The screen recording was made by someone on the growth team for an internal doc. The brand graphic is in a shared drive with 200 other exports. The past-episode moment is in an archive of 60 previous recordings.

**None of it is in the episode file.**

That's the real ceiling on clipping tools, and it's worth being precise about it, because it gets described as a quality problem when it isn't one. Opus Clip, Submagic, Captions, Overlap, these are well-built products that solved a genuine bottleneck. Twenty to forty ranked, reframed, captioned cuts out of a two-hour recording, in about fifteen minutes of review. That used to be most of an editor's day.

But every one of them takes a finished file as input, and that file is the entire world they can see. They can find the best sixty seconds inside it, reframe it, caption it, and pace it. What they cannot do is reach for something that was never uploaded. There's no version of a better model that fixes this. You can't select from material you never ingested.

So the tool does the only thing available: it holds on the speaker's face, and it compensates with motion and typography. Which is exactly the surface-level styling you've been iterating on. The tool is stuck in the same loop you are, for the same reason.

The escape hatch these tools offer is a stock library. Which brings us to the second problem.

## Stock b-roll looks like stock b-roll

Audiences clock it instantly. Not consciously, not as a thought. They just feel the temperature drop.

The founder is talking about how hard the first year of the company was, and up comes the 4K shot of an anonymous team high-fiving in a glass conference room. It's beautifully lit. It's better-shot than anything you own. And it reads as filler, because it's about nothing. Everyone has seen that exact clip under fifty other sentences.

Compare that to a phone video of the actual first office, shaky, badly white-balanced, four people and a folding table. It's worse footage by every technical measure and it's worth ten times more, because it's evidence. The viewer believes the sentence more after seeing it.

This is the line between content that feels like your brand and content that feels like a template. A real shot of the actual thing beats a polished stock alternative almost every time, and the gap widens the more specific the claim being made. Stock can illustrate a category. Only your own footage can prove a fact.

## What to actually do about it

The fix is unglamorous. It's a library.

**Build a small archive of your own reusable b-roll.** Not a huge one. Start with the material you'd reach for most: the product in use, the workspace, footage from events you've run or attended, screen recordings of your own tools and dashboards, your brand graphics and lower thirds, and the standout moments from past episodes. Fifty to a hundred usable pieces covers a surprising amount of what a show actually references.

**Keep it organized and taggable.** An archive nobody can search is an archive nobody uses. Consistent file names, a flat structure, tags by subject rather than by shoot date. The test is whether an editor with no memory of the shoot can find "product close-up, hands on keyboard" in under a minute. If they can't, the library will quietly stop being used by month two.

**Capture b-roll deliberately on shoot days, with clipping in mind.** This is the highest-leverage habit and the one most teams skip. You already have the camera out, the lights up, and the subject in the room. Ten extra minutes gets you the hands, the wide of the room, the product from three angles, the walk-in, the laptop screen. Shoot it knowing it will be cut into vertical clips later, which mostly means: shoot more of it vertical or loose enough to crop, hold each shot longer than feels necessary, and don't move the camera much. Static clean shots cut into anything.

**Treat the archive as an asset that compounds.** Every episode adds to it. This month's screen recording is next quarter's cutaway. A show that's been running two years with a maintained library has an enormous unfair advantage over one starting fresh, not in production value, in specificity.

## The honest part

This is more work up front than changing a caption preset, and there's no way around that. Someone has to shoot the extra material, name it, and put it somewhere findable. The first month is all cost.

But the shape of the payoff is unusually good. The library only grows. Every clip you make from here draws on a bigger pool, so the tenth episode has more to pull from than the first, and the fiftieth has more than the tenth. The cost is front-loaded and fixed; the benefit accrues per clip, forever. Very little else in content production works like that.
